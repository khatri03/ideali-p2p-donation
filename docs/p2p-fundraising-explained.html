Executive briefing

Peer-to-Peer Fundraising, Explained

Instead of one organisation asking thousands of strangers for money, hundreds of supporters each ask the handful of people who already trust them. This document explains how that works, where the money goes, how platforms earn, and what to measure — in plain language, with real examples and no jargon left undefined.

1

What peer-to-peer fundraising actually is

Peer-to-peer fundraising — usually written P2P — is when an organisation asks its supporters to raise money on its behalf, instead of asking the public for money directly.

The organisation creates one campaign. Each supporter then gets their own personal page inside that campaign, with their own photo, their own story and their own target. They share that page with friends, family and colleagues. Those people donate. Every donation, whichever personal page it came through, lands in the organisation's account and counts towards one shared total.

Traditional appeal

The charity does the asking

The charity buys advertising, sends emails, and hopes strangers respond. The message comes from an institution. Cost per pound raised is high, and reach stops where the marketing budget stops.

Peer-to-peer

Supporters do the asking

Two hundred supporters each message twenty people they actually know. The message comes from a trusted friend. Reach grows with the number of supporters, not with the advertising spend.

The one-sentence version: P2P turns supporters into fundraisers, and turns each supporter's personal network into a donor list the organisation could never have bought.

3

The four people involved

Every P2P campaign has the same cast. Names change between platforms; the roles do not.

Organiser

The charity, school or club. Creates the campaign, sets the goal and deadline, owns the bank account that receives every donation, and issues the receipts.

Fundraiser

A supporter who signs up to raise money. Gets a personal page with their photo, story and target. Shares it. Never touches the money.

Team

A group of fundraisers with a shared total — a school class, an office department, a running club. Teams create friendly competition, which produces most of the lift.

Donor

The friend, relative or colleague who gives. Lands on a personal page, donates in under a minute, leaves a message of support, and receives a receipt.

How they nest

  • Campaign — "Spring Walkathon 2026", goal £50,000
    • Team — "Year 6 Falcons", goal £5,000
      • Fundraiser — Aisha, goal £500  £620 raised
      • Fundraiser — Daniel, goal £500  £310 raised
    • Team — "Staff Room", goal £3,000
    • Individual fundraiser — not in any team

A donation to Aisha's page adds to Aisha's total, the Falcons' total and the campaign total at the same moment. One payment, three counters, one bank account.

4

Three real-world examples

Example 1 — Schools

The school walkathon

A primary school wants £50,000 for a new library. The old way: send a letter home and ask parents to donate. Realistic result — a few hundred parents, a few thousand pounds.

The P2P way:

  • The school creates one campaign: Spring Walkathon, goal £50,000, closing in six weeks.
  • Each of 400 pupils gets a personal page: photo, one line on why the library matters, personal goal £125.
  • Each class becomes a team, and a leaderboard shows which class is ahead.
  • Pupils and parents share the link on WhatsApp with grandparents, aunts, neighbours and colleagues.
  • Each pupil converts roughly 8 donors at an average gift of £18.

The school never advertised. The reach came from 400 personal networks that no mailing list contained. And the school now holds contact details for thousands of warm supporters for next year.

Example 2 — Individuals

The birthday fundraiser

Sara turns 30. Instead of presents she asks for donations to an animal shelter. She creates a personal page inside the shelter's always-on campaign, writes two sentences about the rescue dog she adopted there, sets a goal of £300, and posts the link.

Forty-one friends give between £5 and £50. She raises £540. The shelter spent nothing, did no work, and gained 41 donors — most of whom had never heard of it before that morning.

Why it works: the ask carried a personal reason and arrived from someone the donor already cares about. That is the entire mechanism, in miniature.

Example 3 — Corporate

The company marathon challenge

A 900-person company enters a charity marathon. Sixty employees run. Each gets a personal page; each department becomes a team. The company promises to match every pound raised.

  • 60 runners raise £480 each on average, from colleagues, clients and family — £28,800.
  • The company match doubles it — £57,600.
  • The departmental leaderboard runs on the office screens for six weeks.

The charity gets funds plus a corporate relationship. The company gets staff engagement it could not buy. Teams plus a matching pledge is consistently the highest-yielding P2P format.

Pattern across all three: a personal reason, a visible goal, a deadline, and a link that is easy to share. Remove any one of the four and results fall sharply.

5

Where the money actually goes

The most common misunderstanding: people assume the fundraiser collects the money and passes it on. They do not. Money never touches a fundraiser's hands or account.

  1. Donor pays on the personal page

    Card, digital wallet or bank debit. Card details go straight to the regulated payment provider — the platform never stores a card number.

  2. Payment provider authorises and captures

    Stripe, or an equivalent provider, checks the card, runs fraud screening, and takes the payment.

  3. Funds are routed to the organiser's connected account

    Each organisation completes identity and bank verification once, up front. Money is routed to their account, not into a platform pool account.

  4. Processing fee is deducted

    The card networks charge a percentage plus a fixed amount per transaction. This is unavoidable and applies to every online payment everywhere.

  5. Three counters update instantly

    Fundraiser total, team total and campaign total all move at once, and the donor's message appears on the page.

  6. Receipt is emailed to the donor

    Issued in the organisation's name, carrying the details their tax authority requires.

  7. Payout reaches the organisation's bank

    On a rolling schedule, typically two to seven days after the payment, with a statement reconciling every donation in the batch.

Why this matters commercially: because funds route to a verified account belonging to the organisation, the platform is not holding other people's money. That keeps the platform out of money-transmission territory and places the burden of identity checks on the regulated payment provider — a materially different risk and licensing position from a platform that pools funds.

6

How platforms make money

Three models dominate. They differ in who pays, and that difference is the whole competitive story.

Model A

Percentage of donations

The platform takes a cut of every gift — commonly 3–8% — on top of card processing.

Effect: a £100 donation delivers roughly £93 to the cause. Simple and predictable, and increasingly disliked by boards.

Model B

Subscription

A flat monthly or annual licence fee. Donations pass through with only the card processing cost deducted.

Effect: excellent value for high-volume organisations, poor value for small ones paying the same fee to raise very little.

Model C

Zero-fee, donor-funded tip

The organisation is charged nothing. At checkout the donor is invited to add a voluntary contribution that funds the platform.

Effect: the cause can receive 100% of the intended gift. This is the model Zeffy popularised, and it is why it wins price-sensitive nonprofits.

Strategic read: Model C is the strongest message a nonprofit board ever hears — "every pound reaches the cause". Its risk is revenue concentration in donor generosity: tip opt-in rate and average tip percentage become the two numbers the business genuinely lives on, and both move with checkout design.

7

The life of a campaign

Before launch

Set up

Organiser creates the campaign, sets goal and deadline, verifies bank details, writes the story, and prepares the recruitment message.

Weeks 1–2

Recruit fundraisers

The highest-leverage phase. Every extra fundraiser adds a whole network. A campaign that fails almost always failed here.

Week 1

Personalise

Fundraisers add a photo, a reason and a target. Pages with a personal photo and story raise several times more than untouched default pages.

Weeks 1–6

Share and give

Donations arrive in bursts, each burst following a share. Leaderboards, milestones and progress nudges keep sharing alive.

Final 72 hours

The deadline spike

A large share of all donations arrives in the last three days. Deadlines are not decoration — they are the mechanism.

After close

Thank and report back

Thank every donor, publish what the money achieved, and invite the best fundraisers back. This is what turns one campaign into an annual one.

What the platform must provide

The capability checklist a serious P2P product is judged against.

For the organiser

  • Create a campaign with goal, deadline, story and imagery
  • Invite and approve fundraisers and teams
  • Live dashboard: total raised, donor count, top fundraisers
  • Donor export, receipting and refund handling
  • Own logo, colours and domain
  • Role-based access so staff see only what they should

For the fundraiser

  • Sign-up in under two minutes
  • Personal page: photo, story, personal goal
  • One-tap sharing to messaging apps and social networks
  • Progress bar, milestones and a thank-you tool
  • Team leaderboard position

For the donor

  • Give in under 60 seconds, on a phone, without creating an account
  • Card, digital wallet and bank debit
  • Optional recurring gift
  • Leave a public message of support
  • Give anonymously if preferred
  • Immediate emailed receipt

Trust and compliance

  • Card data handled only by the regulated payment provider
  • Identity and bank verification before any payout
  • Fraud screening, refund and chargeback handling
  • Audit trail on every privileged action
  • Data protection: consent captured, data deletable on request
  • Accessible to screen-reader and keyboard users

Mobile is not a nice-to-have. The overwhelming majority of P2P donations start from a link in a messaging app and are completed on a phone, often on a poor connection. A checkout that is slow or awkward on a small screen loses real money, donation by donation.

Glossary

Campaign
The organisation's fundraising drive: one goal, one deadline, one total.
Personal page
A fundraiser's own page inside a campaign, with their photo, story and target.
Team
A group of fundraisers sharing a combined total and a leaderboard position.
Leaderboard
A ranking of fundraisers or teams by amount raised. The main engine of friendly competition.
Matching gift
A pledge, usually corporate, to match donations pound for pound up to a cap.
Connected account
The organisation's own verified account at the payment provider, into which donations are routed.
Payout
The scheduled transfer of settled donations from the payment provider to the organisation's bank.
Processing fee
The card networks' charge per transaction: a percentage plus a fixed amount.
Chargeback
A donor's bank reversing a payment after a dispute. Totals must be adjusted when it happens.
Recurring donation
An automatic repeat gift, usually monthly. The most valuable donor a campaign can produce.

The takeaway in three lines

P2P fundraising works because a request from a friend outperforms an advertisement from an institution. The platform's job is to make asking effortless, giving instant, and money movement completely trustworthy. Win on those three and the fundraising totals take care of themselves.
