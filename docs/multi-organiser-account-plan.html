IDEALI · PLATFORM ARCHITECTURE · PROPOSAL

One Person, Many Charities

Today a single human being can belong to exactly one charity on Ideali. This document explains why, in plain language, shows the exact database structures that cause it, and proposes a fix that adds one new table and changes nothing that already works.

1

The question, and the short answer

Stated plainly before any technical detail.

The question

A person raises money for Charity A. Later, Charity B also asks them to raise money. Can the same person fundraise for both charities using the same Ideali login?

The short answer: no — and not because we forgot to build it

The database physically prevents it. One email address can only ever produce one account, and one account is permanently tied to exactly one charity. This is enforced by a uniqueness rule in the live production database, not by missing code.

Two things follow from that, and they are easy to confuse, so they are separated here:

It is not a peer-to-peer fundraising problem

The fundraising feature itself would handle multiple charities fine. The obstacle sits underneath it, in how accounts and charities are linked. Any feature that needs one person to deal with two charities hits the same wall.

It is fixable without a rewrite

The system already keeps "which charity am I currently working in" in a separate, swappable place — the login token. That single design choice, made long ago, is what makes this fix small instead of enormous. Section 3 explains it.

A trap worth naming

Notice that Contact has a list of Users. It is tempting to read that as "multi-charity support already exists". It does not. That list runs the wrong way: many logins can share one person's details inside one charity. It never allows one login to reach two charities.

The lock: one email, one account, platform-wide

Even if someone tried to work around the chain by creating a second account for the same person, the database refuses. The email column carries a uniqueness rule that covers the entire platform — it is not scoped per charity.

One email address → one login → one contact record → one charity. Fixed, permanent, enforced by the database itself.

Why this matters more than anything else in this document

Because "which charity" lives in the token and not in the chain of tables, changing charity means issuing a new token. It does not mean rewriting queries. All the filtering and stamping described above follows the new number automatically, with no code changes at all. This indirection already exists and is already trusted in production.

One more helpful detail. The function that builds a token set already takes the charity as a plain input rather than looking it up itself:

4

What breaks today — three real stories

Concrete, with names, so the problem is impossible to misread.

Meet Sara. She already has an Ideali account because she fundraises for Hope Trust (charity A). Green Fund (charity B) now wants her too.

Story 1 Sara donates to Green Fund

  1. Sara opens Green Fund's donation page and gives £50.
  2. The system tries to make sure she has an account.
  3. It searches for her email — across all charities, with no filter.
  4. It finds her Hope Trust account and decides she already has one.
  5. It adds a "Donation module" permission to that Hope Trust account and stops.
Result: Green Fund's contact record for Sara has no login attached to it. Sara's account still belongs to Hope Trust.

Story 2 Green Fund invites Sara to fundraise

  1. Green Fund sends Sara an invitation email.
  2. Sara clicks the link and signs in with her usual email and password.
  3. Her token is stamped with Hope Trust, because that is the only charity her account knows.
  4. Every screen now filters to Hope Trust.
  5. Green Fund's campaign is invisible to her. Her fundraising page for it cannot be reached.
Result: the invitation appears to work and then quietly leads nowhere. Nothing displays an error.

Story 3 The security consequence

  1. Green Fund's donation flow triggered the email search in Story 1.
  2. That search matched a record owned by a different charity.
  3. The code then granted a module permission on that other charity's account.
  4. No check was performed that the two charities are the same.
Result: activity under one charity silently modifies an account belonging to another. See section 11.
Verified · the line behind stories 1 and 3 · DonorAccountService.cs:36
var loginInfo = await uow.Repository<User>()
    .Query(false)                                                  // global filters switched OFF
    .FirstOrDefaultAsync(u => contactEmails.Contains(u.UserName));  // no organiser condition

The method receives an organizerId argument. This particular lookup never uses it. The match is on email alone, across every charity on the platform.

5

Two ways to fix it, one rejected

Both are written out, so the choice can be judged rather than trusted.
Rejected

Option A — let one person have several accounts

Change the uniqueness rule from "email is unique on the platform" to "email is unique within a charity". Sara then gets two separate accounts with the same email address, one per charity.

Why this is rejected

  • Login becomes ambiguous. Sara types her email and password. Which of her two accounts is she signing into? The system cannot know. Every sign-in screen would have to ask "which charity?" before she can even authenticate.
  • A live production index must be dropped and replaced. That is a destructive change to a shipped table, which is exactly what we are trying to avoid.
  • Two passwords, two profiles, two password resets for one human being. Sara changes her password and it only changes for one charity.
  • It multiplies accounts to solve a problem that is really about membership.
Recommended

Option B — one account, several memberships

Keep exactly one account for Sara. Add a new table that records which charities that one account belongs to. Sara stays one person with one password, and simply has two memberships.

Why this is the right shape

  • Login never changes. Email still identifies one human. The unique index stays.
  • Only a new table is added. No existing column is altered or dropped.
  • It matches reality. Sara is one person who supports two causes — not two people.
  • It fits the existing design. The token already carries "current charity"; this simply gives that value more than one legal setting.
  • Nothing happens until a second membership exists. Every current user has one, so every current user behaves identically.

The idea in one sentence

Stop treating "which charity" as a permanent property of the account, and start treating it as a membership the account can hold more than one of — while the token continues to decide which membership is active right now.

Constraints and indexes

RuleWhy it is there
Unique (UserId, OrganizerId) A person can join a charity once, not twice. Without this, a duplicate row would double every count that walks memberships.
Unique (ContactId) One contact record belongs to one membership. Prevents two different people's logins claiming the same contact row.
Index (UserId, IsDeleted) The commonest question — "which charities does this person belong to?" — is asked on every sign-in and every switch.
Foreign keys to User, Contact, Organizer A membership pointing at a charity that does not exist is corrupt data. The database should refuse it.
Delete behaviour: restrict Deleting a charity must not silently erase membership history. It should fail loudly and be handled deliberately.

Critical design rule — do not mark this table IOrganizerFilter

Almost every table in this system carries IOrganizerFilter, and doing so is normally correct. Here it would be a serious bug.

Remember from section 3 that IOrganizerFilter makes every query add WHERE OrganizerId = <current charity> automatically. The whole point of this table is to answer "which other charities does this person belong to?". If the table filtered itself to the current charity, it would only ever return the one you are already in — the exact answer that is useless. This table must be queried unfiltered, with permission checked explicitly in code instead.

How switching charity works

One new endpoint. No query anywhere else changes.

Everything in this section is Proposal.

Sara is signed in and currently working in Hope Trust. She picks Green Fund from a menu. Here is exactly what happens:

  1. The frontend calls the new endpoint

    POST /api/identity/account/switch-organizer with the target charity's uniqueId in the body. The current access token is sent as normal.

  2. The server checks permission — this is the security gate

    It looks for a live UserContact row matching the signed-in user and the requested charity. If there is no such row, the request is refused. Nothing about roles is consulted; the membership row itself is the permission.

    Deny by default. No row, no switch. The user id comes from the token, never from the request body, so a caller cannot ask to switch on someone else's behalf.

  3. A completely new token set is issued

    The existing GenerateTokenDataAsync is called with the target charity. Both the access token and the refresh token are new. The old refresh token is revoked, so the previous charity's session cannot be resumed with it.

  4. Everything else follows automatically

    The middleware reads the new stamp, the repository filter uses the new number, and new rows get stamped with the new charity. No query, filter, or service was modified to make this work — that is the payoff from section 3.

Proposal · the permission check, written out
var membership = await uow.Repository<UserContact>()
    .Query(false)                          // deliberately unfiltered — see the design rule in section 6
    .FirstOrDefaultAsync(uc =>
        uc.UserId == claimStore.UserId &&  // from the token, never from the request
        uc.Organizer.UniqueId == request.OrganizerUniqueId &&
        !uc.IsDeleted,
        cancellationToken);

if (membership is null)
{
    return Forbidden();                    // no membership, no switch. No detail leaked about why.
}

Three things that must not be forgotten

ThingWhy, and what to do
The refresh token Verified RefreshToken implements IOrganizerFilter and GenerateRefreshTokenAsync takes organizer.Id. A refresh token therefore belongs to one charity. Issuing only a new access token would leave the old refresh token in place, and the next silent refresh would drop the user back into the previous charity. Both tokens must be replaced, and the old refresh token revoked.
The live connection Verified AlertHub.cs:53 reads the charity from the token when the connection is established. An open connection keeps the old charity forever. The frontend must disconnect and reconnect after a switch.
Anything cached in the browser Charity name, logo, branding, permissions and menu items are held after sign-in. All of it is wrong the moment the charity changes. The simplest correct behaviour is a full page reload after a successful switch, which clears every cache at once and costs the user one second.

Migration, step by step

Each step is safe on its own and can be stopped after.

Everything in this section is Proposal. The order matters, so it is numbered and each step states what can be undone.

  1. Fix the cross-charity email lookup first

    Described fully in section 11. It must come first, because building memberships on top of a lookup that links accounts by accident would turn a quiet bug into permanent, legitimate-looking cross-charity access.

    Undo: revert the change. It is a code fix only, no data is touched.

  2. Create the UserContact table

    A brand-new, empty table. No existing table is read, locked, or altered. This is the safest kind of migration there is.

    Undo: drop the table. Nothing depends on it yet.

  3. Fill it from the data that already exists

    One row per existing user, built from the values already on their record. No new information is invented and nothing is guessed.

    Proposal · the backfill
    INSERT INTO dbo.UserContact (UserId, ContactId, OrganizerId, IsDefault, JoinedOnUtc, IsDeleted)
SELECT  u.Id,
        u.ContactId,
        c.OrganizerId,
        1,                      -- every existing membership is that user's default
        SYSUTCDATETIME(),
        0
FROM    dbo.[User]   u
JOIN    dbo.Contact  c ON c.Id = u.ContactId
WHERE   NOT EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM dbo.UserContact x WHERE x.UserId = u.Id);

    After this runs, every user on the platform has exactly one membership — which is precisely what they effectively had before. Nothing has gained a second one.

    Undo: delete all rows. No other table was written to.

  4. Verify the backfill before going further

    Three counts must match expectations: total memberships equals total users; every user has exactly one row marked default; no user has zero rows. If any count is wrong, stop — do not proceed to step 5.

    Undo: nothing to undo. This step only reads.

  5. Add the switch endpoint and the frontend menu

    The endpoint from section 7, plus a charity switcher in the user menu that only appears when a person has more than one membership. Since every user currently has exactly one, no user sees any difference at this point. The feature is live but dormant.

    Undo: remove the endpoint and hide the menu. No data changes.

  6. Allow a second membership to be created

    Only now does anything actually become possible that was not possible before. The first flow to use it should be a single, deliberate one — accepting an invitation from a second charity — rather than every flow at once.

    Undo: stop creating new rows. Existing single memberships are unaffected.

The property that makes this safe

Steps 2, 3 and 5 change nobody's experience. The platform behaves identically after each of them. The first genuine behaviour change happens at step 6, and even then only for a person who actually has a second membership. Work can pause after any step and sit there indefinitely without harm.

Before running any generated migration

Open the generated migration file and read it before it touches a real database. Confirm it only creates a new table, and does not alter, rebuild, or re-index User or Contact. A tool that decides to rebuild a large shipped table can hold locks for a long time. Reading the file takes a minute and removes the risk entirely.

Frontend changes

Small, and invisible to anyone with a single charity.

Everything in this section is Proposal.

WhatDetail
The sign-in response carries a membership list The existing response gains a list of the charities this account belongs to — name, unique id, and which one is currently active. For a person with one membership the list has one item.
A charity switcher in the user menu Rendered only when the list has two or more items. With one item, nothing is rendered at all — not a disabled control, not a single-item dropdown. A menu that cannot do anything should not be on screen.
One service function Added to the existing auth service, calling the switch endpoint through HttpClient so the standard auth header and refresh behaviour apply. No direct axios call.
Switching shows real feedback The control is disabled and shows a loading state while the request is in flight, using the standard Chakra v2 isDisabled and isLoading props. On success, store the new tokens and reload the page. On failure, show a plain message and leave the user where they were.
A designed screen for the collision case When someone is invited by a second charity and their account cannot yet join it, they must see a real explanation — not a raw error and not a silent success. This screen is worth building before the rest of this plan lands, because the situation happens today. See section 12.

Do not store the membership list as a long-lived cache

Memberships change when a charity adds or removes someone. A copy kept in the browser goes stale and there is no mechanism to refresh it. Read it from the sign-in response, use it to draw the menu, and let the server decide every actual permission. As with roles elsewhere in this platform, what the browser holds decides only what is drawn on screen — never what is allowed.

11

Security fix required before any of this

Written in full, without compression, because it matters.

The defect

DonorAccountService.EnsureDonorUserAccountAsync looks up a user by email address with the repository's global filters explicitly switched off and with no organiser condition in the query. When a donation is made under organiser B, and an account with that email already exists under organiser A, the method matches organiser A's account and then grants it a UserModule row for the Donation module.

The two organisers are never compared. Activity performed under one tenant modifies an account owned by a different tenant, without any authorisation check.

Verified · DonorAccountService.cs:36
var loginInfo = await uow.Repository<User>()
    .Query(false)
    .FirstOrDefaultAsync(u => contactEmails.Contains(u.UserName));

if (loginInfo != null)
{
    var existingModule = await uow.Repository<UserModule>()
        .Query(false)
        .FirstOrDefaultAsync(u => u.UserId == loginInfo.Id && u.ModuleId == (byte)EnumModule.Donation);

    if (existingModule == null)
    {
        await uow.Repository<UserModule>().AddAsync(new UserModule
        {
            UserId = loginInfo.Id,
            ModuleId = (byte)EnumModule.Donation
        });
        await uow.SaveChangesAsync();
    }

    return;
}

Why this must be fixed before the membership work, not after. Right now this is a quiet bug: an unintended link between two tenants. Once UserContact exists, cross-tenant linkage becomes a legitimate, supported concept. If this lookup is still in place at that point, an accidental email match could create a real membership — a permanent, authorised-looking grant of access to another charity's data, produced by nothing more than two people sharing an email address or one person donating twice.

The fix. Resolve the account within the organiser that the donation was actually made under. The method already receives organizerId; the lookup must use it. If an account with that email exists under a different organiser, that is not a match — it is a distinct situation that must be handled deliberately and explicitly, never by silently reusing the other tenant's account.

Related items found in the same area

These are separate from this plan and are recorded here only so they are not lost: a commented-out organiser condition in the donor contact upsert path; and lockout fields on User (FailedAttempts, IsLocked, LockoutDateUtc) that exist in the schema but which no code ever sets, meaning account lockout is not actually enforced. Both deserve their own review.

12

Relationship to peer-to-peer fundraising

What ships now, and what changes later.

The peer-to-peer fundraising project does not wait for this plan. The two are sequenced, not blocked on each other.

Peer-to-peer version one

  • Ships with one fundraiser account belonging to one charity.
  • This is stated openly as a known limitation, not hidden.
  • The limitation belongs to the platform's account model, not to the fundraising feature.
  • The fundraising tables themselves already allow one person to have rows under several charities — they are not the obstacle.

The one thing that must be built now

The collision must be a designed screen, not an error and not a silent failure.

When someone accepts an invitation from a second charity and their email already belongs to an account under a different charity, they must be shown a clear explanation of what is happening and what they can do. Story 2 in section 4 is what happens without this screen: the invitation appears to work and then leads nowhere.

Nothing built now is thrown away later

Once UserContact exists, that same collision screen stays exactly where it is. Only its button changes — from an explanation that this is not currently possible, to "Add this charity to your account", which creates a membership row. Same screen, same route, same place in the flow. One button swapped.

Decided 21 August 2026 — where the active charity comes from

A person who belongs to several charities has to be doing something on behalf of one of them at any moment. That charity is read from the route, not from the token, and it is named by Organizer.UniqueId — the existing Guid — never by the integer Organizer.Id.

  • Why the route and not the token. The OrganizerId claim is read at 199 call sites across every module and is the platform’s tenant boundary. Making that claim switchable would put a cross-charity data leak behind all 199 of them, and it would force a diff inside the five locked identity files. Reading the charity from the route leaves every one of those call sites, and all five files, untouched.
  • Why the Guid and not the integer. Sequential integers in a URL let anyone count the charities on the platform and walk them one by one. The Guid carries no ordering and no count. This is also already the convention here — DonateController routes on {campaignUniqueId:guid}, and every entity on the platform implements IUniqueId.
  • Integers stay server-side. A new endpoint resolves the route Guid to the internal int once, uses it for queries, and never returns it. No integer identifier appears in a new URL or a new response body.
  • The Guid identifies; it does not authorise. Knowing a charity’s Guid grants nothing. After resolving it, the endpoint requires an accepted UserContact row joining the signed-in user to that charity, and denies by default when there is none. This check is server-side on every request.
  • New endpoints only. Existing endpoints keep scoping by the claim exactly as they do today. Nothing already shipped starts reading the route.

One thing this needed that did not exist yet — now built. Organizer.UniqueId is defaulted with NEWID() but carries no unique index — unlike most other tables here, which do. Looking a charity up by Guid is a table scan today. So a filtered unique index on Organizer (UniqueId) lands before any route uses it. That is an index creation on a shipped table: it adds no column, rewrites no row, and needs a maintenance window because a non-online index build takes a lock on Organizer while it runs.

Built 21 August 2026. The index is configured at BaseDbContext.Configuration.cs inside ConfigureOrganizer, and the migration 20260820200630_AddOrganizerUniqueIdUniqueIndex lives in Ideas.Identity.Persistence — the only migration set that owns the Organizer table. The generated migration creates one index and nothing else, and the model snapshot moved by exactly three lines, so no unrelated model drift rode along with it. Two things still have to happen before it is applied to production, in this order. First, run the duplicate check below; a unique index cannot be created if two rows already share a UniqueId, and rows that predate the NEWID() default could hold 00000000-0000-0000-0000-000000000000. Second, apply it inside a maintenance window, because a non-online build holds a lock on Organizer for its duration.

SELECT UniqueId, COUNT(*) AS Rows
FROM dbo.Organizer
GROUP BY UniqueId
HAVING COUNT(*) > 1;

SELECT Id, Name
FROM dbo.Organizer
WHERE UniqueId = '00000000-0000-0000-0000-000000000000';

Decided 21 August 2026 — who may end a membership

Both sides may end it. A person may leave a charity, and a charity may remove a person. Membership is consent-first — the charity invites and the person accepts — and consent that cannot be withdrawn is not consent. A charity equally should not have to wait for someone who has stopped participating.

  • The home charity cannot be left. Self-removal applies only to additional memberships. Sign-in reads u.Contact.Organizer through User.ContactId, and that is the source of the OrganizerId claim, so leaving the home charity would break the person’s ability to sign in at all. Moving a home charity is a separate operation with its own rules, not a self-service button.
  • Ending is a status change, never a delete. The UserContact row moves to a revoked state. Past donations, DonationCampaignInvoice rows and fundraiser pages stay attached to the charity, because they are financial and tax records and may not be erased by someone leaving. Ending a membership removes the ability to raise money for that charity from that point onward; it rewrites nothing behind it.
  • The last administrator cannot leave. If the person is that charity’s only remaining owner or administrator, the leave is refused with a clear message until another one exists. Otherwise a charity can be left with nobody able to administer it.
  • Both directions are authorised server-side. A person may end only their own membership; a charity may end only memberships to itself, and only through an account holding an administrative capability there. Both are audit-logged: who ended what, when, and against whom.

Re-joining later is a fresh invitation and a fresh acceptance. A revoked row is never silently reactivated.
